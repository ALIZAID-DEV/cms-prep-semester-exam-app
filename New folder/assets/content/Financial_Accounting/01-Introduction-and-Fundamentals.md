Definition of Accounting
• Accounting is the process of identifying, recording, classifying, summarizing, and reporting
financial transactions.
• It provides useful information for decision-making.
• It follows accounting principles.
• It helps measure business performance.
• Example: Recording cash sales.

What is Financial Statement
• Financial statements are formal reports showing the financial performance and position of a
business.
• They are prepared at the end of an accounting period.
• They help owners and investors.
• Main statements include income statement and balance sheet.
• They follow accounting standards.

Types of Financial Statements
• The main financial statements are Income Statement, Statement of Retained Earnings, Balance
Sheet, and Cash Flow Statement.
• Each serves a different purpose.
• They summarize financial data.
• They support decision-making.
• They are prepared periodically.

Managerial vs Financial Accounting
• Financial accounting prepares reports for external users.
• Managerial accounting provides internal information for managers.
• Financial accounting follows standards.
• Managerial accounting is flexible.
• Both help business decisions.

Organization Types
• A sole proprietorship has one owner.
• A partnership has two or more owners.
• A corporation is a separate legal entity.
• Each has different legal and accounting features.
• Ownership affects reporting.

Accounting Cycle
• The accounting cycle starts with transactions and ends with financial statements.
• Steps include journalizing, posting, trial balance, adjustments, statements, and closing.
• It repeats every period.
• It ensures accuracy.
• It is a common exam topic.

Users of Financial Accounting
• Users include owners, investors, creditors, banks, government, employees, and management.
• They use reports for decisions.
• Investors check profitability.
• Banks assess creditworthiness.
• Government checks taxes.

Accounting Equation
• Assets = Liabilities + Capital.
• Revenue increases capital.
• Expenses and drawings reduce capital.
• Retained earnings accumulate profits.
• Current assets include cash and receivables.

Analysis of Transactions
• Every transaction affects at least two accounts.
• The accounting equation remains balanced.
• Analyze increase or decrease in accounts.
• Identify debit and credit.
• Example: Cash purchase of equipment.

General Journal
• The general journal records transactions in chronological order.
• It is the book of original entry.
• Each entry has date, accounts, debit, credit, and narration.
• Accuracy is important.
• Entries are later posted.

Ledger
• A ledger contains individual accounts.
• Transactions are posted from the journal.
• It shows account balances.
• It helps prepare the trial balance.
• It is the book of final entry.
Trial Balance
• A trial balance lists all ledger balances.
• Total debits must equal total credits.
• It checks arithmetic accuracy.
• It is prepared before adjustments.
• Errors may still exist.

Adjusted Entries
• Adjusting entries update revenues and expenses.
• They record accruals and prepayments.
• They are made at period end.
• They ensure matching principle.
• Then they are posted.

Adjusted Trial Balance
• Prepared after adjustments.
• It contains updated balances.
• It is the basis for financial statements.
• Debits equal credits.
• It improves accuracy.

Financial Statements
• Income Statement shows profit or loss.
• Statement of Retained Earnings shows changes in retained earnings.
• Balance Sheet shows assets, liabilities, and equity.
• They summarize performance.
• They are prepared from the adjusted trial balance.

Closing Entries
• Closing entries transfer temporary account balances to retained earnings/capital.
• Revenue and expense accounts are closed.
• Drawings are also closed.
• New period starts with zero temporary balances.
• Permanent accounts remain open.

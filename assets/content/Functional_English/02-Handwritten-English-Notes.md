## Grammar and Language

**Functional English** develops four pillars: reading, writing, listening, and speaking. Grammar is the set of rules that governs the structure of language, including words, phrases, clauses, and sentences. Language is a process of sharing and connecting with others.

**Analyze** means breaking something down into its parts. **Synthesis** means combining elements to create something new.

## Sentences, Phrases, and Persons

A **sentence** is a word or group of words that makes complete sense and meaning. Examples include “I study in the morning,” “She bought a beautiful computer,” and “The cat is under the table.”

A **phrase** is a group of words that does not express a complete thought. Examples include “in the morning,” “a beautiful computer,” and “under the table.”

There are three persons: **first person** refers to the speaker, such as I and we; **second person** refers to the listener, such as you; and **third person** refers to the person or thing being spoken about, such as they, these, and those.

## Types of Sentences

There are five main types of sentences: declarative, interrogative, imperative, optative, and exclamatory.

A **declarative sentence** makes a positive or negative statement and ends with a full stop. An **interrogative sentence** asks a question and uses a question mark. An **imperative sentence** expresses a command or request. An **optative sentence** expresses a wish, blessing, or curse. An **exclamatory sentence** expresses strong feeling or emotion and uses an exclamation mark.

## Nouns

The main kinds of nouns are common, proper, countable, uncountable, collective, compound, abstract, concrete, and material nouns.

A **common noun** names a general thing. A **proper noun** specifies a particular person, place, or thing. A **countable noun** has singular and plural forms, such as man/men, chair/chairs, ox/oxen, child/children, and woman/women. An **uncountable noun** does not normally have a countable plural form, such as water, milk, sugar, tea, oil, salt, and petrol.

A **collective noun** names a collection, such as a library of books, a family of members, a bouquet of flowers, or a team of players. An **abstract noun** names an idea, quality, feeling, or state that cannot be physically touched, such as love, respect, truth, hate, and emotion. A **compound noun** is formed from two or more words, such as blue pen, whiteboard, and Sindh Road Transport Corporation. A **concrete noun** refers to something that can be seen or touched. A **material noun** names a substance from which things are made.

## Gender

**Masculine gender** refers to males, **feminine gender** refers to females, **common gender** can refer to either male or female, and **neuter gender** refers to things that are neither male nor female.

Examples of masculine/feminine pairs include drake/duck, tomcat/cat, peacock/peahen, salesman/salesgirl, chairman/chairperson, landlord/landlady, and priest or monk/nun.

## Alphabet and Parts of Speech

An alphabet is a set of letters. Vowels are a, e, i, o, and u. Consonants are the other basic units of the alphabet. Letters may be capital or small.

Parts of speech classify words: a **noun** is a naming word; a **pronoun** is used instead of a noun; a **verb** expresses an action or state; an **adverb** modifies a verb, adjective, or another adverb; an **adjective** adds meaning to a noun; a **conjunction** joins words or sentences; an **interjection** expresses sudden feeling; and a **preposition** shows the location or position of a noun.

## Communication

Communication is the process of sharing or exchanging information, ideas, thoughts, or feelings between people or systems. A simple definition is: **communication = sending + receiving + understanding a message**.

Verbal communication uses speaking or talking. Non-verbal communication uses body language, facial expressions, and gestures. Written communication uses letters, emails, and messages. **Intrapersonal communication** is communication with oneself, such as thinking or reflecting. **Interpersonal communication** is the exchange of ideas, feelings, or information between two or more people. A **monologue** has one speaker, while a **dialogue** involves two or more people exchanging ideas.

## Paragraph Writing

A paragraph is a group of related sentences focused on one idea or topic. It normally includes a topic sentence, supporting sentences, and a concluding sentence. Paragraphs organize thoughts, improve clarity, and provide a foundation for essays and articles.

An informative paragraph explains a topic with clear, factual information. Common practice topics include pollution, sports, natural disasters, and other familiar subjects. A good paragraph has a clear topic sentence, relevant supporting details, and a conclusion.

## Punctuation

Punctuation marks separate sentences and their elements and clarify meaning. Important marks include the apostrophe, brackets or parentheses, colon, comma, exclamation mark, full stop, hyphen, question mark, semicolon, quotation marks, and ellipsis.

Use commas to separate three or more items in a series. Parentheses enclose extra information. An apostrophe shows possession or a contraction, such as “Sarah’s bag.” Brackets can add clarifying or editorial notes. Quotation marks show quoted words. A semicolon connects related independent clauses, while a colon introduces an explanation or list.

## Prepositions

A **preposition** tells where, when, how, or in what way something happens. Common examples are:

- **in** — inside something: The book is in the bag.
- **on** — touching a surface: The pen is on the table.
- **at** — a specific place or time: Meet me at the bus stop.
- **to** — moving toward: She walked to school.
- **from** — a starting point: I came from Lahore.
- **into** — going inside: The cat jumped into the box.
- **onto** — moving onto a surface: Put the cup onto the shelf.
- **over/above** — higher than something.
- **below/under** — lower than or beneath something.
- **between** — in the middle of two things.
- **among** — in the middle of many things.
- **behind** — at the back of something.
- **in front of** — before something.
- **beside/near** — next to or close to something.
- **by** — next to, using, or before something.
- **with** — together with.
- **without** — not having.
- **for** — purpose or benefit.
- **of** — belonging to.
- **about** — concerning.
- **since** — from a past time until now.
- **until/till** — up to a certain time.

Types of prepositions include prepositions of place, time, direction, manner or means, and cause or purpose.

## Prefixes, Suffixes, and Word Formation

An affix is a group of letters attached before or after a root word. A **prefix** comes before the root word; a **suffix** comes after it.

Common prefixes include dis (not or opposite), inter (between), mis (wrong), non (not), pre (before), re (again), sub (under), tri (three), and un (not). Common suffixes include able (can be done), en (made of), er (one who), est (most), ful (full of), less (without), ly (in a manner), ment (action of), and ness (being). Examples include unacceptable, uneasy, uncomfortable, irregular, childish, kindness, reflection, rejection, and unbearable.

## Articles

Articles modify or describe nouns. The definite article is **the**. The indefinite articles are **a** and **an**. Use “a” before a consonant sound and “an” before a vowel sound. Articles are generally not used with plural proper nouns or uncountable nouns in the general sense.

## Letter Writing and Dialogue

A letter is a written message. Formal letters include business and official letters; informal letters include social or personal letters.

A dialogue should have an appropriate opening or salutation, a suitable tone, clear characters and situation, grammatically accurate related sentences, correct punctuation, and a proper ending. Practice situations include a shopkeeper and customer bargaining and two friends planning a trip.

## Sentence Structure

A sentence has two main parts: the **subject**, which names the person or thing being discussed, and the **predicate**, which tells something about the subject. A predicate may contain a verb, object, complement, and extension of the predicate.

A simple sentence expresses one complete thought. A compound sentence joins two independent clauses, often with a coordinating conjunction such as for, and, nor, but, or, yet, or so. A complex sentence contains an independent clause and a dependent clause.
